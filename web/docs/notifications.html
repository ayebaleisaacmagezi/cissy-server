How push works

Notifications that arrive when your app is closed cannot come from the app itself. They come from your server, through Google's delivery network, to the phone. Web2App is not in that path.

The path a notification takes

Your website or backend
        ↓
Firebase Cloud Messaging  (Google)
        ↓
   ┌────┴────┐
Android     APNs  (Apple)
              ↓
           iPhone

Web2App's part happens at build time: it puts the Firebase code into your app and wires your configuration into it. Once your app is built, Web2App can be switched off entirely and your notifications keep arriving.

That is deliberate, and it is worth understanding

If every notification had to travel through us, we would become a permanent dependency of your business - our uptime would be your uptime, our rate limits your rate limits, and our bill would eventually be your bill. Sending directly means none of that is true.

Why you need a Firebase project

Firebase Cloud Messaging is how Android and iPhone deliver notifications to an app that is not running. There is no way around it, and it is free at the volumes almost everybody sends.

The project is yours. You create it on your own Google account. We never ask for your password, and we never see inside it. If you stop using Web2App, the project, the apps registered in it and the ability to send stay with you.

What Web2App holds, and what it does not

We hold the two client configuration files - google-services.json and GoogleService-Info.plist. These are not secrets: they ship inside every copy of your app, and anyone who downloads it can read them.

The server credential - the service-account key that can actually send a message - never comes to us. You keep it on your own server. Same for the Apple push key, which you upload straight to Firebase.

Two kinds of notification, often confused

What it isCan it fire when the app is closed?
Push Your server sends a message that travels to the phone. Yes. This is the one that needs Firebase.
Local The app schedules something for later on the phone itself. Only for things the phone already knows about - a reminder somebody set. It cannot know an order shipped.

If you want to tell people about something that happened on your server, you want push.

Categories

Rather than tracking individual phones, you send to a category and Firebase delivers it to everybody subscribed. You define the categories in Studio - General, Offers, Order updates, whatever suits - and each one gets a name your backend sends to.

Your users can turn categories on and off inside the app, so somebody who wants order updates but not marketing can have exactly that. Nobody has to choose between all of it and none of it, which is what makes people turn notifications off entirely.

Category names are permanent once you publish

Installed apps subscribe by name. Rename a category after release and every phone already subscribed silently stops receiving it, with nothing anywhere reporting a problem. Change the label freely; leave the name alone.

Messages meant for one person

Categories are broadcasts. For "your order is ready" you need to reach one person, which means your backend has to know which phone belongs to which user. There are two ways to arrange that, both covered in Sending.

What happens when somebody taps one

A notification can carry a page to open. The app opens it inside itself, after checking it is on your allowed domains - a notification cannot send your users to somebody else's site, and it cannot make the app do anything other than open a page.

That works whether the app was open, in the background, or completely closed. A tap that starts the app from cold still lands on the right page.
